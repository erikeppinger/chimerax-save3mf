Printing protein models

This guide takes one protein from the PDB to a multi-colour print, step by step, and then collects tips for each kind of model and the slicer settings that matter for Save3MF files. In ChimeraX it is part of the help (help 3mf, then Printing protein models), where you can click every command to run it.

Have a tip that worked for you? This page is meant to grow – Contributing a tip says how.

Worked example: ubiquitin (PDB 1ubq)

You will use three tools:

  • Save3MF, to save the model with its colours;
  • the NIH 3D print presets, to make it printable (install from Tools → More Tools…);
  • optionally Support Optimizer, to find the best orientation. It runs in the browser or as a desktop app.

All the steps are also in one script: examples/1ubq-print.cxc.

1. Open the structure

open 1ubq

Any PDB ID or local file works. For a protein made of several copies, open the biological assembly if that is what you want to print.

2. Make it printable

preset "nih3d" "ribbon by secondary structure (printable)"

You can also pick a preset from the Presets menu. The printable presets make thin parts thick enough to print and add supports that hold the model together: hydrogen bonds drawn as thick rods and, where needed, extra struts.

Good to know: the presets delete the water molecules, and the ribbon presets also delete the hydrogens. show cannot bring them back, and a session saved afterwards won't have them. Your original file is not changed; open it again to get them back. If a particular water matters, save the session before running a preset.

3. Check the supports

Most supports help, but now and then one sits badly – pinning the end of the chain at an odd angle, say, or creating an overhang. Hover over a support to see which two atoms it joins, then delete it by naming them. In 1ubq, for example:

delete pbonds /A:1@N /A:17@O

Other ways to do it:

  • Click instead of typing: Ctrl+click the support to select it, then delete pbonds sel.
  • Keep it, but leave it out of the print: hide it instead, hide /A:1@N /A:17@O pbonds. Only what is shown gets saved.
  • Careful with whole residues: delete pbonds /A:1,17 removes every support between those two residues, not just one.

Thinner supports. If the supports look too heavy, make them thinner:

size pseudobondRadius 0.4

This sets all supports to a radius of 0.4 Å. It keeps their colours, and supports you deleted stay deleted. To change only the hydrogen bonds, name their model: size #1.1 pseudobondRadius 0.4 (or #2 for a struts model). Avoid hbonds radius … or struts … radius … for this: they recalculate the supports and bring deleted ones back.

Don't make them too thin. In the print, a 0.4 Å support at 100 mm is about 2.3 mm across; keep supports at least 1.2–1.5 mm thick (three widths of a 0.4 mm nozzle).

Why? A support's thickness in the print is 2 × radius × scale, and the scale (mm per Å) is in the log when you save: about 2.9 mm/Å for 1ubq at 100 mm. A support is also attached to the ribbon only by its own cross-section, so its strength drops with the square of the radius – half the radius, a quarter of the strength.

4. Colour it – then recolour the supports

color /A cornflowerblue
color strand gold
color helix tomato

These commands colour the supports too, so they now blend into the ribbon. Give them their own colour as the last colouring step:

color lightgray target p halfbond false

target p colours only the supports (hydrogen bonds and struts alike), and halfbond false gives each one a single colour instead of two halves. Repeat this after any later plain colour command such as color /A red. Colour schemes – color bychain, rainbow, color byattribute – leave the supports alone.

Tip: each colour costs a filament change on every layer it appears in. A support colour that already occurs in the model – the grey of the loops, say – costs nothing extra.

5. Preview colours, print time and smoothness

3mf palette maxColors 4 size 100 tools 4

This saves nothing. It shows the four colours, how much printing time the filament changes take on a 4-filament printer, and whether saving at this size will smooth the model.

6. Save

save ~/Desktop/1ubq.3mf size 100 maxColors 4
  • size 100 – the longest side becomes 100 mm. (scale sets mm per Å instead.)
  • maxColors 4 – merge into at most four colours. Set it to the number of filaments your printer has; gradients such as rainbow or B-factor need it.
  • flavor – prusa (the default) for PrusaSlicer, bambu for Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer, fullcolor for full-colour printers.
  • parts chain – one part per chain, for complexes (see Complexes). 1ubq has only one chain.

The log then lists the colours and what the printer will make of the model. Watch the scale: well below 1 mm per Å, supports can get too thin to print. Large prints are smoothed automatically – 1ubq at 100 mm needs nothing, at 200 mm the supports and ribbons get rounder.

Good to know: saving takes a moment for big models. Sticks, balls and spheres of a large structure are millions of triangles: haemoglobin as sticks takes about half a minute, ubiquitin a few seconds. ChimeraX has no progress bar and doesn't respond until it is done, but the status line at the bottom of the window shows what it is working on.

7. Orientation and cavities in Support Optimizer (optional)

Drag the 3MF file in; the colours are kept.

  • Surface models only: Find cavities, then Fill selected cavities. Otherwise support material gets trapped inside where you can never remove it. Skip this for ribbon and strut models.
  • Set the overhang angle your printer manages (about 45° for most FDM printers). For models above about 20,000 triangles, enable Use low-res proxy. Then Optimize orientation.
  • Compare to start model and Show support paths show whether the new orientation needs less support.
  • Export 3MF for multi-colour, or Export STL for one colour.

8. Slicer

  • Open the 3MF. The colours arrive already assigned to filaments – as long as you saved with the flavour for your slicer: prusa for PrusaSlicer, bambu for Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer.
  • Assign your filaments, and use organic or tree supports.
  • Don't re-orient the model if Support Optimizer already did.
  • Check the preview: no small loose bits, supports printed without gaps.
  • Check the two slicer settings for multi-colour prints below.

Tips by representation

All models

  • Size. By default 1 Å becomes 1 mm, far too small for most proteins. Ribbons and bonds usually need 2–3× that to print reliably.
  • Thickness. Keep supports, bonds and ribbon edges at least 1.2–1.5 mm thick (three nozzle widths). Models that will be handled are safer at 3 mm, and models over 20 cm at 6 mm.
  • Smoothness. Large prints are smoothed automatically (see Smoothness).
  • Remove what you don't need before saving: water, unwanted chains, loose ions. For a surface, delete atoms rather than hide them – a hidden atom still shapes the surface around it. (A hidden support, on the other hand, is simply left out.) Print a ligand separately if it should come out.
  • Orientation. Automatic orientation (Support Optimizer, or Meshmixer's Orientation) noticeably cuts down overhangs and support material.
  • Support material. Organic or tree supports suit protein shapes. Leave places you could never reach unsupported, such as the inside of a helix: short overhangs there usually bridge. A second material as support interface (PETG under PLA, for example) makes removal much easier.
  • Flat base. If the model rocks or touches the bed at a single point, cut 1–2 mm off the bottom with the slicer's cut tool.
  • Multi-colour. Filament changes cost more time than coloured area. Merge colours with maxColors (preview with 3mf palette), reuse model colours for the supports, and use the wipe tower, plus purging into infill or the object if your slicer offers it.
  • Mesh check. Closed meshes avoid slicer errors; repair in the slicer or in MeshLab if needed.

Smoothness in large prints

ChimeraX draws for the screen: a support has 10 flat sides, a ribbon about a dozen. Printed large, those flat sides show. A 0.6 Å support printed at 200 mm has flat sides that sit 0.17 mm inside a true circle – almost a whole 0.2 mm layer.

So when you save, Save3MF checks how deep those flat sides would be at your print size. Where they are deeper than 0.05 mm, it saves the supports, bonds, atoms and ribbons with more sides. The log says so first, including the extra file size, and your view in ChimeraX stays as it was. For 1ubq at 200 mm, the support flats go from 0.17 to 0.04 mm and the file from 0.5 to 1.0 MB; at 100 mm nothing changes.

To check in advance, without saving anything:

3mf palette size 200

You can change the target when saving:

  • save model.3mf size 200 smoothness 0.1 – coarser: fewer triangles, a smaller file
  • save model.3mf size 200 smoothness 0.02 – finer
  • save model.3mf size 200 smoothness off – exactly what is on screen

In the Save dialog this is the Smooth for printing option.

Surfaces of molecules and maps are not changed: how fine they are is set when ChimeraX computes them. If a surface is too coarse for your print size, the log tells you. Make a molecular surface finer with, for example, surface gridSpacing 0.3, or show a map at full resolution with volume step 1.

Complexes of several chains

A complex – say an AlphaFold prediction of two proteins, or a dimer from the PDB – normally arrives in the slicer as one piece. To handle each chain on its own, save one part per chain: save complex.3mf size 100 parts chain flavor bambu.

  • The slicer shows one object with a part per chain (chain A, chain B, …). You can select, hide or set up each one separately, and it keeps its colours.
  • The chains stay where they are. (As separate objects, the slicer would drop each one onto the bed.)
  • A surface that covers several chains – made with surface … enclose, or by the NIH surface presets – stays one part, surface of chains A, B: split along the chains, it would have holes. For a separate surface per chain, run surface close and then surface before saving.
  • parts model does the same per model, for structures opened side by side.
  • Use the flavour for your slicer (prusa or bambu). With the wrong one, the file opens but not as intended: Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer show a prusa file as one part without colours, and PrusaSlicer splits a bambu file into separate objects.
  • To print the chains as separate pieces, split the object in the slicer (OrcaSlicer: right-click → Split → To objects). This drops the colours, so assign a filament to each piece afterwards. Tiny extra pieces from the split are hollow spaces inside the protein and can be deleted.

In the Save dialog this is the Parts option.

Surface models

  • The easiest kind of model to print on an FDM printer.
  • Fill hollow spaces inside first (Support Optimizer), or support material gets trapped.
  • A smaller probe radius (0.5–1.4 Å) shows more detail; a finer grid gives a smoother surface but a bigger file. Very large structures may need to be split into several surfaces.
  • Chain colours print as painted areas; see the slicer's segmented region width.
  • Large complexes such as virus shells: print subunits or halves separately and join them with pins or magnets. That makes printing and support removal easier, and putting them together is great for teaching.

Cartoon and ribbon models

  • Make the ribbons thicker (cartoon style … thickness, or struts … fattenRibbon true) and print 2–3× larger.
  • Struts are essential. A typical choice is struts @ca length 7 loop 30 radius 0.8 fattenRibbon true; the NIH presets add supports for you. For DNA and RNA: struts @C5' length 15 loop 80 radius 1.
  • Hydrogen bonds shown as thick rods (radius about 0.5 Å) also hold sheets and helices together.
  • After a preset, check the supports, and recolour them after a plain colour command (steps 3 and 4). If they look too heavy, make them thinner – but not below what the print size allows.
  • In Support Optimizer, skip the cavity search for ribbons; orientation and support preview work as usual.
  • Very delicate ribbons often print better on resin (SLA) or powder (SLS) printers. On FDM, print slower and use organic supports.

Space-filling and ball-and-stick models

  • Sphere models behave like surfaces and print well. Touching spheres fuse in the print; the log counts parts closer than about 0.4 mm as one.
  • In ball-and-stick, the sticks are the weak point: make them thick, print larger, and split big molecules into parts if needed.
  • Round undersides need support. A flat cut at the bottom, or a few atoms chosen as a base, reduces it.

Materials

Three plastics cover almost everything a protein model needs:

  • PLA is made from plant starch or sugar. It is the easiest to print – low temperature, little warping, crisp detail – and comes in the most colours. It is stiff but brittle, and softens at about 60 °C, so a model left in a hot car can bend. Despite being plant-based, it only breaks down in industrial composting. The usual choice for teaching and display.
  • PETG is related to the plastic of drinks bottles. It is tougher than PLA, bends a little before it breaks, and copes better with heat and chemicals, but strings more while printing. Good for models that get handled a lot.
  • TPU is soft and rubbery. It prints slowly and needs care, but makes parts that bend and spring back – for example a flexible joint between two printed parts.

Which ones combine:

  • PLA and PETG hardly stick to each other. That makes PETG ideal as support interface under PLA: the supports snap off cleanly. For the same reason, a model made of both needs beam interlocking to hold together.
  • TPU only with beam interlocking and a suitable partner: PETG with TPU works, PLA with TPU poorly.

Slicer settings for multi-colour prints

Two settings act on painted colours, so they matter for every multi-colour Save3MF file. PrusaSlicer, OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio all have them, under the same names and with the same defaults; type "interlocking" into the settings search to find them. The times below were measured with PrusaSlicer 2.9.6 on an Original Prusa XL profile (0.20 mm layers, 0.4 mm nozzle).

Maximum width of a segmented region

How deep a painted colour goes into the model. The Original Prusa XL profiles set 5 mm; PrusaSlicer's own default is 0, meaning all the way through.

  • 5 mm: each colour is a 5 mm shell around a core of a neighbouring colour. It looks the same from outside and saves filament changes.
  • 0: colours go all the way through. Use this if the model will be cut open to show solid chain colours inside.

Interlocking depth of a segmented region (greyed out while the width is 0) varies the shell depth from layer to layer so that shell and core grip each other.

Beam interlocking

Where two colours meet, this weaves them into each other with thin crossed beams a few millimetres deep, hidden under the surface. It was made for materials that don't stick to each other (PLA with PETG, rigid with TPU), but on protein models it is needed even when all colours are the same plastic. It works on painted colours as well as separate parts.

What to do:

  • Turn it on for multi-colour protein models, even in a single material, and expect 17–42 % more printing time.
  • Start with the defaults and print a small test piece – a single joint of the model is enough – before the whole model. If it doesn't hold, raise the depth first and leave boundary avoidance for last: that setting is what keeps the weave hidden under the surface.
  • Keep boundary avoidance at 2 or more on visible models; below that the stripes can show on the surface.
  • Give the weave room. Thin parts – ribbons, supports, narrow contacts between chains – are often thinner than the hidden zone, so the weave disappears or shows through there (reported as PrusaSlicer issue #15011). Thicker supports and ribbons, or a larger print, help. It works best on surface models with large contact areas.
Variant Print time Tool changes
Interlocking off (two-part test block) 26 m 02 s 64
On, defaults (0.8 mm, 22.5°, 2 layers, depth 2, avoidance 2) 30 m 30 s (+17 %) 64
Painted object with horizontal colour bands, off → on 40 m 07 s → 56 m 49 s (+42 %) 32 → 44

Experience from a real print. A 1ubq ribbon model in PLA on an Original Prusa XL showed both sides of it. Without interlocking, joints between two colours – a loop meeting a β-strand, say – held only briefly and then broke apart, because walls printed one after the other by different tools fuse only weakly. With interlocking at the defaults, PrusaSlicer often failed to connect the parts properly; settings strong enough to hold – depth 3, boundary avoidance 1 – pushed each colour into its neighbour where it showed on the surface. The parts of such a model are only a few millimetres thick, while the weave needs cells twice the beam width (1.6 mm by default). The feature is still experimental, and it shows here.

Contributing a tip

Tips that worked on a real print are welcome – a setting, a material combination, a ChimeraX command, a mistake worth avoiding.

  • Describe it: open a printing tip issue. Say what you printed, on which printer and slicer, and what made the difference; a photo helps.
  • Or edit this page: it is docs/printing-protein-models.md; open a pull request. See CONTRIBUTING for the details.

Tips are checked before they are added, and ChimeraX commands are run, so everything on this page has worked at least once. Please add the source if the tip comes from somewhere else.
